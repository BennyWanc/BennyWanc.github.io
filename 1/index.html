Images of the Russian Empire

CS280A Project 1 --- Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection

Overview

Each picture contains three grayscale exposures in B, G, R order. I held blue fixed, aligned green and red to blue, and stacked the aligned channels into an RGB image. The movement below use (x, y), which means x and y moves for x,y axis.

Single-scale alignment

For the small JPEGs, I searched translations in a 31×31 window centered at (0, 0). For every photo, I shifted the channel with np.roll, ignored the outer image area, and measured normalized cross-correlation (NCC). The candidate with the highest NCC was selected.

Multi-scale pyramid

Searching the full shifting range on large TIFFs would be hard and time consuming. I resized each channel by half until its largest dimension is at most 400 pixels. I search ±15 pixels at the coarsest level, double that displacement at each finer level, and refine it within ±2 pixels. The TIFF results take about four seconds each on my machine.

Additional collection images

I selected three additional full glass-plate scans from the Prokudin-Gorskii collection and processed them with the same fixed parameters.

CS280A bells and whistles

Better features

Raw pixels are unreliable when the same material has very different brightness through different filters. I compute gradient magnitude, √(dx²+dy²), and align those edge images with NCC. The shifts are then applied to the original channels. This substantially improved Emir: its robe, face, and architectural edges overlap more closely.

Automatic cropping

I first use the measured shifts to retain only the region shared by all three aligned channels. Within the outer 15% of the image, I mark dark pixels, bright pixels, and pixels with a large difference between color channels as likely border artifacts. I crop through the last suspicious row or column when more than 35% of it is marked. This detects the actual scan borders instead of removing a fixed margin.

Automatic contrast

I map the 0.5th intensity percentile to black and the 99.5th percentile to white, clipping values outside that range. Percentiles prevent a few defects or scan-border pixels from controlling the entire contrast stretch.

Automatic white balance

I use the gray-world assumption: the average scene color estimates the illuminant. Each channel is scaled toward the shared mean, using half of the full correction to avoid overly aggressive casts. It helps some scenes, such as Icon, but can make Emir slightly greener because its scene average is not neutral.

Better color mapping

The historical glass-plate filters do not exactly match modern RGB channels, so I apply a small 3×3 linear color transformation. Each output channel keeps most of its original value, receives a slight boost, and subtracts a small contribution from the other two channels. This gently separates the colors while keeping the mapping stable for every image. The matrix is a simple visual correction rather than a calibrated recovery of the original filter responses.

Final Results

These images use the complete pipeline: gradient-feature pyramid alignment, automatic overlap and border cropping, gray-world white balance, color mapping, and percentile contrast.

Limitations

A single translation cannot correct motion between the three exposures, so moving people, foliage, and water may retain local ghosting. Border detection also leaves some colored bands when their values are not sufficiently dark or bright. Gray-world white balance depends on the scene's average color and therefore gives mixed results. Despite these limitations, the edge-based pyramid produces stable global alignment across the provided images and three additional scans.
